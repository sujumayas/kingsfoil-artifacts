# 00a. Glossary, theory side

Scope. The theory vocabulary for the Curves series. Every later study reuses these terms without redefining them.
Tags follow CONTEXT.md. [V] verified from the cited source, [I] inference, [U] unverified or contested, [K] Kingsfoil fact.
Grades: E1 data, E2 designer or author statement, E3 community consensus, E4 anecdote. For academic papers, E1 marks the
paper's own measured result and E2 marks the author's framing or argument.

Method note. Primary PDFs were read in full text where reachable (MDA, Chen thesis, Hunicke & Chapman 2004, Juul 2009,
EOMM, Booth 2009, Lazzaro 2004, Ryan et al. 2006, Linehan et al. 2014, Denisova et al. 2017 and 2020, Denisova & Cairns
2015, Koster's "10 years later" deck). Books (Csikszentmihalyi, Schell, Juul 2013, Koster 2004) were not readable here.
Book claims rest on publisher pages, author decks or reviews and are tagged so.

## Q1. Flow. What is the "flow channel" and how far can we lean on it?

### Takeaway
Flow is the right word for one specific state, deep absorption when challenge and skill are both high and matched.
It is the wrong word for "fun" in general. The series should say "flow channel" only for the challenge-skill
diagram, and should treat it as a necessary-not-sufficient condition. Chen's useful addition is that players can
steer themselves inside the channel through choices embedded in play, which fits Hearthguard better than hidden DDA.

### Cited findings
- Chen restates the channel. Challenge above ability "generates anxiety"; challenge below ability "provokes boredom". [V, E2] [S1]
- Chen lists Csikszentmihalyi's eight components of flow: a challenging activity that requires skill, merging of
  action and awareness, clear goals, direct feedback, concentration, sense of control, loss of self-consciousness,
  transformation of time. He adds "Not all of these components are needed". [V, E2] [S1]
- Chen's thesis argues system-side DDA fails for four reasons: no direct data on the player's state, "performance is
  objective while Flow is subjective", assumptions that do not hold for a mass audience, and changes fixed by the
  designer's taste. [V, E2] [S1]
- Chen's alternative, "Active Flow Adjustment". Expand the range of difficulty on offer, let players choose, and
  "embed choices into the gameplay" so choosing does not break absorption. flOw is his test bed (the player dives to
  a harder layer or rises to an easier one). [V, E2] [S1]; the dive mechanic description is [I] from the thesis
  structure and common knowledge of flOw, not quoted.
- Chen's thesis also names the four-part DDA loop: Player, Monitor, Analysis, Game System. [V, E2] [S1]
- Csikszentmihalyi's later 8-channel model (credited to Massimini & Carli 1988, popularised in Finding Flow, 1997)
  splits the challenge-skill plane into arousal, flow, control, relaxation, boredom, apathy, worry and anxiety.
  [U, E3] [S4]. I could not reach the original chapter or the book; secondary pages disagree on where arousal and
  control sit. Treat the 8 labels as approximate.
- A 2020 PeerJ review of flow in games states the channel model plainly and notes that balance is necessary but
  does not guarantee flow. [V, E2] [S3]
- Sweetser & Wyeth's GameFlow (ACM Computers in Entertainment, 2005) maps game heuristics onto flow as 8 elements:
  concentration, challenge, player skills, control, clear goals, feedback, immersion, social interaction, with
  38 criteria. They validated it only by expert review of two RTS games, one high-rated and one low-rated. They note
  immersion is hard to assess because it describes the player, not the game. [V, E2] [S5]

### Critiques (where flow misleads)
- Flow is one aesthetic among several. MDA lists 8 aesthetics, and flow maps mostly onto Challenge. [V, E2] [S10]
  Lazzaro's field study found popular games create emotion in at least three of four keys, and only Hard Fun is
  the challenge-skill one. [V, E1] [S23]
- Juul found players rated a game highest when they failed some and then won, and lower when they never failed
  (p=0.06, near significant). A perfect challenge-skill match with no failure is not the target. [V, E1] [S8]
- Robb & Zhang (2022) found a DDA group performed better but reported no significant difference in enjoyment or
  flow. [V, E1] [S31] (from the abstract as quoted by search; full text not read)
- Boredom and anxiety are not symmetric costs. [I] Juul's data suggest players forgive difficulty they blame on
  themselves (p<0.016) [V, E1] [S8], while boredom has no such forgiveness channel.
- "Flow" in designer talk often means "pacing felt smooth". That is Schell's interest curve or Booth's intensity
  cycle, not Csikszentmihalyi's state. [I]

### Inferences
- [I] For Hearthguard, the Horde wave (w4) is designed to push players out of the channel into anxiety on purpose.
  The channel diagram would call that a design error. Juul and Booth explain why it is not. [K] wave 4 is "three surges
  faster than they can be cleared" [S32].
- [I] Chen's embedded-choice idea already exists in Hearthguard as calling waves early (Space, 2 gold per second
  skipped) [K] [S32]. That is a player-steered difficulty dial with a reward, the cleanest form of Active DDA.

### Gaps
- Original Massimini & Carli 1988 chapter and Finding Flow (1997) not read. Chen's 2007 CACM article
  ("Flow in Games (and Everything Else)") not read; the thesis is the fuller source.
- Thesis year not printed in the extracted text. USC MFA thesis, 2006, is [I].

## Q2. Fun as learning. Koster, and why mastered games go dull

### Takeaway
Koster's claim is that fun comes from learning patterns and stops when nothing is left to learn. Use it as the
series' explanation for boredom and for why the late game needs new patterns, not bigger numbers. Do not use
"fun = learning" as a full definition. Koster himself credits the line to Chris Crawford and the book is an essay.

### Cited findings
- Koster's own deck quotes the book: "Fun in games arises out of mastery" and "learning is the drug". [V, E2] [S6]
- The deck credits Chris Crawford with the earlier form, fun is "the emotional response to learning". [V, E2] [S6]
- The deck frames games as pattern-learning systems, with fun as the reward for keeping at it. It reports the book sold
  over 30,000 copies. [V, E2] [S6]
- Grokking, Koster's term for understanding deep enough to be automatic. Boredom follows full mastery. [V, E3] [S6]
  (grok and boredom details via book summaries surfaced in search, not the book text)
- First edition 2004 (Paraglyph), second edition 2013 (O'Reilly). [I] from publisher listings; search sources disagreed.

### Where it misleads
- [I] It underweights MDA's non-learning aesthetics (Fellowship, Sensation, Submission). A co-op game with friends can
  stay fun after the patterns are known.
- [I] "Learning" here covers skill learning and system learning. In Hearthguard, the power curve (talents, Town)
  can create new patterns without any new enemy, which Koster's frame handles only loosely.

### Gaps
- Book text not read. Grok and boredom passages come from summaries [E3].

## Q3. Failure. Juul's punishment types and the paradox of failure

### Takeaway
Adopt Juul's four punishment types as the series' vocabulary for "cost of failure". They split Hearthguard's costs
cleanly. Hero death is energy-to-life punishment with a timer; the Hearth falling is game termination plus a full
setback to chapter 1. Adopt "failure as information" too. Juul's data show failure players blame on themselves
lifts ratings.

### Cited findings
- "Fear of Failing? The Many Meanings of Difficulty in Video Games", in The Video Game Theory Reader 2, Routledge,
  2009, pp. 237-252. [V, E2] [S8]
- Method. A Pac-Man/Snake hybrid with 4 levels and 3 lives. 9 offline testers, then 85 online players (73 male). [V, E1] [S8]
- Players who lost some lives then finished rated the game highest; flawless players rated it lower (p=0.06). [V, E1] [S8]
- Players who said "the game was too hard" rated it lower than those who blamed their own mistakes (p<0.016). [V, E1] [S8]
- Punishment types [V, E2] [S8]
  - Energy punishment. Losing energy, moving toward losing a life.
  - Life punishment. Losing a life, moving toward game over.
  - Game termination punishment. Game over.
  - Setback punishment. Replaying part of the game or losing abilities.
- Juul's phrase "failure adds content". Failure makes players rethink strategy, so the game seems deeper. [V, E2] [S8]
- Juul suggests casual games appeal by sparing setback punishment more than by sparing energy punishment. [V, E2] [S8]
- The Art of Failure (MIT Press, 2013). Paradox of failure, as quoted by reviewers: we avoid failure, we fail in
  games, and we still seek out games. [V, E2] [S9] (via review and publisher page, book not read)
- Juul argues game failure is personal. You, not a character, were inadequate. [V, E3] [S9] (reviewer paraphrase)

### Where it misleads
- [I] The 2009 sample is small and 86% male, with one prototype. Use the categories confidently; use the effect sizes
  cautiously.
- [I] Setback punishment hides two variables that matter for Hearthguard. Distance lost (chapter vs run) and time
  lost (minutes to get back). The series should record both.

### Gaps
- Art of Failure chapter text not read.

## Q4. MDA and Hunicke's DDA work

### Takeaway
MDA is the series' default lens for "which knob causes which feeling". Use its 8 aesthetics names as given.
Hunicke's Hamlet is the canonical early DDA paper and introduced the inventory view (supply and demand of health
and ammo), which is cheaper to tune than enemy stat scaling.

### Cited findings
- MDA by Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek, taught at the GDC Game Design and Tuning Workshop 2001-2004. Paper is from the AAAI
  2004 Workshop on Challenges in Game AI. [V, E2] [S10] (workshop venue is [I], not printed in the extracted text)
- Mechanics, "data representation and algorithms". Dynamics, "run-time behavior of the mechanics". Aesthetics,
  "desirable emotional responses evoked in the player". [V, E2] [S10]
- Designers read M to D to A; players read A to D to M. [V, E2] [S10]
- 8 aesthetics, verbatim glosses. Sensation (sense-pleasure), Fantasy (make-believe), Narrative (drama), Challenge
  (obstacle course), Fellowship (social framework), Discovery (uncharted territory), Expression (self-discovery),
  Submission (pastime). The list is "not limited to" these. [V, E2] [S10]
- MDA's own Quake example adds "Competition", which is not in the list of 8. [V, E2] [S10]
- Hunicke & Chapman, "AI for Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment in Games" (Northwestern, 2004). Hamlet is built on the
  Half-Life engine and uses Inventory Theory to manage supply and demand of health, ammo and similar items. [V, E2] [S11]
- Hamlet's trigger is "flailing", repeated inventory shortfalls that predict failure. [V, E2] [S11]
- Hunicke, "The case for dynamic difficulty adjustment in games", ACE 2005, Valencia, pp. 429-433,
  doi 10.1145/1178477.1178573. [V, E2] [S12]. Reviews say Hamlet adjusted reactively (NPC accuracy, damage) and
  proactively (spawned NPC health and damage). [V, E3] [S12 via search]

### Where it misleads
- [I] MDA's arrow suggests mechanics determine aesthetics. Chen's and Denisova's work shows framing and belief change
  the aesthetic too (see placebo, Q5).
- [U] The often-repeated claim that ACE 2005 players "did not notice" Hamlet could not be verified. Do not repeat it
  without reading the paper.

### Gaps
- ACE 2005 full text (results, sample size) not read.

## Q5. DDA, player modelling, and the "DDA paradox"

### Takeaway
"DDA" in the series means any automatic change to challenge based on measured player state. Split it into stat DDA
(change enemy numbers), supply DDA (change health and ammo, Hamlet), and pacing DDA (change when threats arrive,
L4D). Hearthguard's director already chose pacing over stats [K] [S32]. The "DDA paradox" is a useful name but the
evidence is mixed, so the series should qualify it. Hidden adjustment can raise immersion; visible or suspected
adjustment hurts autonomy and fairness.

### Cited findings
- Zohaib, "Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment (DDA) in Computer Games: A Review", Advances in Human-Computer Interaction,
  2018, doi 10.1155/2018/5681652. Frames DDA as real-time change to match skill so players are neither bored nor
  overwhelmed. [V, E2] [S13] (abstract only)
- Player modelling, per Yannakakis et al. 2013, is "detection, modeling, prediction and expression" of player
  characteristics. The 2018 book (Yannakakis & Togelius) devotes a part to modelling players; the 2025 second edition
  moves it to chapters 10-12. [V, E2] [S14, S15] (chapter text not read)
- L4D AI Director (Booth, Valve, 2009). "Adaptive Dramatic Pacing". Each survivor has an intensity value that rises
  with damage taken, incapacitation, ledge pulls and nearby kills, and decays when no infected engage. [V, E2] [S17]
- Director states. Build Up until intensity crosses a peak; Sustain Peak 3-5 s; Peak Fade until intensity decays;
  Relax 30-45 s or until survivors travel far enough. Inspired by Counter-Strike's "spiky" pacing; "constant,
  unchanging combat is fatiguing". [V, E2] [S17]
- Special infected spawn at randomized 90-180 s intervals on Normal. [V, E2] [S17]
- EOMM (Chen, Xue, Kolen, Aghdaie, Zaman, Sun, Seif El-Nasr; EA and Northeastern; WWW 2017). Matchmaking that
  minimises churn risk instead of pairing equal skill. Data, 36.9 million matches by 1.68 million players, first half
  of 2016. [V, E1] [S16] (vendor data, simulated not live)
- EOMM finding. 7-day churn after three losses was 5.1% vs 2.6-2.7% for safer states. EOMM retained 0.7% more players
  per round than skill matchmaking, about 15% more over 20 rounds (compounded). Skill matchmaking did not reliably
  beat random matchmaking. [V, E1] [S16]
- Denisova & Cairns 2015a. 42 participants; a timer silently adjusted to performance. The adjusted group reported
  more immersion (IEQ). Players were not told. [V, E1] [S19]
- Denisova & Cairns 2015b, placebo. 21 participants played Don't Starve twice; once told maps were random, once told
  an "adaptive AI" shaped them. Maps were always random. Players' perception changed anyway. [V, E1] [S20]
- Smeddinck et al., CHI 2016. 40 participants compared menu, embedded and automatic difficulty. Most preferred manual
  choice; overall experience changed little; adjustment type affected perceived autonomy. [V, E1] [S18] (abstract via search)
- Rubber-banding. Racing AI that speeds up behind and slows down ahead, or item odds tied to position (Mario Kart).
  Developers say it draws criticism when it is visible, and some rename it to escape the reputation. [V, E3/E4] [S21]

### Where it misleads
- "DDA paradox" (players like being helped until they notice) [U]. No single paper names or proves it. The pieces are
  E1 but small: hidden DDA raised immersion (n=42), belief alone shifted perception (n=21), visible choice is
  preferred (n=40). Use the term only with this qualifier.
- [I] DDA as a word implies stat changes. Booth's director changes only when threats arrive, which players read as
  drama, not help. The series should keep "pacing director" separate from "DDA".
- [I] EOMM optimises retention, not fun. Its numbers are vendor-reported and simulated. Do not cite it as proof
  that unfair matches feel good.

### Gaps
- ACE 2005 and Zohaib full text, and the book chapter, not read. No large controlled study of disclosed vs hidden DDA
  found (one Aalto master's thesis exists, abstract only).

## Q6. Skill atoms, learning curves, learnability

### Takeaway
Cook's skill atom is the series' smallest unit of learning and should be canonical. Linehan et al. give the
measurable shape of a good learning curve in puzzle games. Introduce one skill alone, in a simple setting, then
practise and combine it, raising complexity until the next skill arrives. That is the sawtooth, with data behind it.
Kishōtenketsu is the same idea told as a four-beat level, and should be called a level structure, not theory.

### Cited findings
- Cook, "The Chemistry of Game Design", Gamasutra, 19 July 2007. Skill atom loop is action, simulation, feedback,
  modeling (the player updates a mental model). A skill chain is "a directed graph of atoms". [V, E2] [S22]
- Burnout. A mastered skill with no further use. Early burnout cuts off later atoms; late burnout lets feeder skills
  atrophy. Pre-existing skills are the chain's roots; teaching what players already know is boring. [V, E2] [S22]
- Cook proposes logging atom states (mastered, partial, unexercised, active, burned out) per playtester. [V, E2] [S22]
- Linehan, Bellord, Kirman, Morford, Roche, CHI PLAY 2014, doi 10.1145/2658537.2658695. Coded play-through videos of
  Portal, Portal 2 co-op, Braid and Lemmings using behaviour-analysis metrics (new actions vs new combinations,
  steps to solve). [V, E1] [S24]
- Their four-part finding. Skills introduced separately; introduced in puzzles needing few other actions; then practised
  and integrated with earlier skills; complexity rises until the next new skill. [V, E1] [S24]
- Later work notes Linehan et al. did not observe how players reacted to the curves. [V, E2] [S24 via search]
- Lazzaro, "Why We Play Games: Four Keys to More Emotion Without Story" (XEODesign, 8 March 2004). Field study of
  30 adults plus 15 non-players, 45 hours of video. Keys are Hard Fun (challenge, frustration, fiero), Easy Fun
  (curiosity), Altered States (players' internal feelings), The People Factor (social). [V, E1] [S23]
- PLAY heuristics, Desurvire & Wiberg, HCII 2009. Categories include Game Play, Skill Development, Tutorial, Strategy &
  Challenge, Game/Story Immersion, Coolness. Successor to HEP (Desurvire, Caplan, Toth, CHI 2004). [V, E2] [S25]
- Kishōtenketsu. Koichi Hayashida (Super Mario 3D Land director) cited this four-part structure from Chinese poems and
  four-panel comics. GMTK (Mark Brown, 2015) popularised it as introduce, develop, twist, conclude. Levels each
  centre on one gimmick (e.g. World 2-2 folding panels). [V, E2] [S26] (via secondary coverage, interview text not read)

### Where it misleads
- [I] "Learning curve" in common speech means "hard to learn" (a "steep curve"). In the learning sciences, a steep curve
  means fast learning. The series should not say "steep" without stating the axes.
- [I] Linehan's data are puzzle games with discrete solutions. Real-time games add execution skill, so practice
  segments need repetition under pressure, not only new combinations.
- [I] Kishōtenketsu is a structure Hayashida described; calling it Nintendo's "method" overstates it.

### Gaps
- Cornett 2004 (CHI, MMORPG usability) and Microsoft Games User Research learnability work not retrieved. Steve Swink
  ("Game Feel", 2008) not checked; it is about control feel, not difficulty, and is probably out of scope.

## Q7. Curve vocabulary, challenge taxonomies, SDT

### Takeaway
Keep three curves separate. Learning curve (what the player knows and can do over time), difficulty curve (the
challenge the game presents over time), power curve (the player's means over time, from levels, talents, Town).
Felt difficulty is challenge minus power, read through the player's skill. Schell's interest curve is the pacing
frame for a session. CORGIS gives the series four kinds of challenge and a validated questionnaire. SDT (competence,
autonomy, relatedness) and the PENS survey explain why choice and co-op matter beyond the challenge-skill match.

### Cited findings
- Schell, The Art of Game Design (2008), Lens #61 "The Interest Curve". Asks for a hook, rising interest with periodic
  rests, a finale that beats what came before, fractal structure, and playtester-drawn curves compared with intent.
  [V, E3] [S27] (lens text via a lens-card transcription, book not read)
- Booth's Build Up / Peak / Relax cycle is the measured version of tension and release. [V, E2] [S17]
- Denisova, Guckelsberger, Zendle, CHI 2017 late-breaking work, doi 10.1145/3027063.3053209. Reviewed challenge items in
  9 existing questionnaires and found them shallow, fragmented, sometimes unvalidated. Proposed physical, cognitive and
  emotional challenge. [V, E2] [S28]
- CORGIS (Denisova, Cairns, Guckelsberger, Zendle, IJHCS 2020). Factor analysis with N=394 found four components,
  performative, emotional, cognitive, decision-making. Validated with nearly 1,000 players. [V, E1] [S29]
- Ryan, Rigby, Przybylski, Motivation and Emotion 30(4), 347-363, 2006, doi 10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8. Four studies.
  In-game autonomy and competence predict enjoyment, preference and well-being change. Relatedness predicts enjoyment
  in an MMO survey. [V, E1] [S30]
- The same paper introduces PENS (Player Experience of Need Satisfaction) with subscales for in-game competence,
  autonomy, presence, and intuitive controls. [V, E1] [S30]
- Perceived vs actual difficulty. The placebo study shows framing alone moves perceived difficulty and immersion
  [V, E1] [S20]; Juul shows attribution (self vs game) moves ratings [V, E1] [S8].
- Yerkes-Dodson (inverted U, arousal vs performance, 1908 mouse study). [U] Not researched here. Designers cite it
  loosely for "some stress helps". Flag it as folk use.

### Where it misleads
- [I] "Difficulty curve" plotted from enemy stats alone ignores the power curve. For Hearthguard, mob HP per chapter
  [1.0, 2.3, 4.6, 8.0, 13.0] means nothing without hero damage +9% per level [K] [S32].
- [I] Interest curves are drawn after the fact by designers. Schell's own check is to have playtesters draw them.
- [I] SDT competence is not "winning". Ryan et al. tie it to optimal challenge and positive feedback.

### Gaps
- Schell book text, Yerkes-Dodson original, and any designer source tying it to a curve not checked.

## Pattern cards found

### Card T1. Player-steered difficulty (Active DDA)
- Lineage. Chen thesis and flOw (2006) -> Smeddinck et al. 2016 measured preference for manual choice -> (players keep
  autonomy while difficulty still adapts).
- Feeling. Control, "I chose this".
- Mechanism. A frequent in-play choice that trades risk for reward, with no menu.
- Works when the choice is legible and repeated. Breaks when one option always dominates.
- Evidence. [V, E2] [S1]; [V, E1] [S18].
- Cost S. Knobs: reward rate, frequency of the choice.
- Hearthguard. Early wave call already does this [K]. Candidate to extend to the Horde wave or fires.

### Card T2. Intensity-gated pacing director
- Lineage. Counter-Strike's natural spiky pacing -> L4D Director (Booth 2009) -> (adapts timing, not stats).
- Feeling. Tension and relief.
- Mechanism. Intensity value per player; Build Up, Sustain Peak 3-5 s, Peak Fade, Relax 30-45 s.
- Works with continuous spawning. Breaks with fixed scripted waves unless applied to gaps.
- Evidence. [V, E2] [S17].
- Cost M. Knobs: intensity weights, peak threshold, relax length.
- Hearthguard. The existing director paces without touching stats [K]; the 12 s gap could flex on intensity.

### Card T3. One-skill-then-combine sawtooth
- Lineage. Behaviour analysis -> Cook skill chains (2007) -> Linehan et al. 2014 measured it in four puzzle games.
- Feeling. Mastery, "I get it".
- Mechanism. New skill alone in a low-step setting, then practise and combine until the next skill.
- Works for discrete skills. Breaks if new skills pile up in one wave.
- Evidence. [V, E1] [S24]; [V, E2] [S22].
- Cost S. Knobs: gap between introductions, steps per encounter.
- Hearthguard. Map each QWER rank, talent row, fire and the dark as atoms; check one per chapter.

### Card T4. Supply-side difficulty (Hamlet inventory)
- Lineage. Hunicke & Chapman 2004 -> ACE 2005.
- Feeling. Quiet rescue, no visible cheating.
- Mechanism. Detect "flailing" (repeated resource shortfalls); adjust supply of health or other items.
- Works where items flow. Breaks when players notice drops tied to failing.
- Evidence. [V, E2] [S11].
- Cost M. Knobs: shortfall threshold, supply delta.
- Hearthguard. Fires heal rings and campfire refills are a supply channel [K]; relight timing could flex.

## Proposed glossary entries

Status codes. ADOPT = canonical term. QUALIFY = use with the stated caution. AVOID = do not use in series text.

| Term | Plain definition (≤25 words) | Source | Use / caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flow | Deep absorption where action and awareness merge; needs high, matched challenge and skill, clear goals, direct feedback. | [S1, S3] | QUALIFY. A state, not a synonym for fun. Balance is necessary, not sufficient. |
| Flow channel | The band on a challenge vs skill chart between anxiety (too hard) and boredom (too easy). | [S1] | ADOPT for the diagram only. Deliberate spikes outside it can be good design. |
| 8-channel flow model | Challenge-skill plane split into arousal, flow, control, relaxation, boredom, apathy, worry, anxiety. | [S4] | QUALIFY. Labels from secondary sources; not verified against the original. |
| Active DDA / player-steered difficulty | Difficulty that adapts through choices the player makes inside play, not through hidden system changes. | [S1] | ADOPT. Preferred over hidden DDA for Hearthguard. |
| GameFlow | Sweetser & Wyeth's 8-element, 38-criterion heuristic checklist for enjoyment, mapped onto flow. | [S5] | QUALIFY. Expert-review checklist, weak validation (two RTS games). |
| Fun as learning | Koster's claim that games are fun while players are learning their patterns; mastery ends the fun. | [S6] | QUALIFY. Explains boredom; ignores social and sensory fun. Credit Crawford. |
| Grok | Understanding a pattern so well it becomes automatic. | [S6] | ADOPT as Koster's term for full mastery. |
| Paradox of failure | We avoid failure, games make us fail, and we seek games anyway. | [S9] | ADOPT as framing only. |
| Energy punishment | Losing health or energy, moving toward losing a life. | [S8] | ADOPT. |
| Life punishment | Losing a life or retry, moving toward game over. | [S8] | ADOPT. In Hearthguard, hero death with respawn timer. |
| Game termination punishment | The run ends. | [S8] | ADOPT. Hearthguard: Hearth falls. |
| Setback punishment | Losing progress or abilities and replaying part of the game. | [S8] | ADOPT. Always record distance lost and minutes lost. |
| Failure as information | Failure that shows the player what to change, so it adds depth; ratings rise when players blame themselves. | [S8] | ADOPT. Small sample (85); trust direction, not size. |
| MDA | Mechanics (rules, code) produce dynamics (runtime behaviour) that produce aesthetics (player feelings). | [S10] | ADOPT as default lens. Framing can also change aesthetics. |
| MDA aesthetics | Sensation, Fantasy, Narrative, Challenge, Fellowship, Discovery, Expression, Submission. | [S10] | ADOPT the names verbatim. The list is open-ended. |
| DDA | Automatic change to challenge based on measured player performance or state, during play. | [S12, S13] | QUALIFY. Always say which kind: stat, supply or pacing. |
| Supply DDA (Hamlet) | Adjusting the flow of health, ammo or items when the player shows repeated shortfalls ("flailing"). | [S11] | ADOPT. |
| Pacing director | System that varies when and how many threats arrive from a player intensity estimate, without changing enemy stats. | [S17] | ADOPT. Keep separate from DDA. |
| Intensity (L4D) | A per-player number raised by damage and danger, decaying when no enemies engage; drives the director. | [S17] | ADOPT. |
| Build up / peak / relax | Director cycle. Full threat until intensity peaks, hold 3-5 s, fade, relax 30-45 s. | [S17] | ADOPT as the measured form of tension and release. |
| Rubber-banding | Opponents or rewards scale with the player's position, helping those behind and holding back leaders. | [S21] | QUALIFY. Accepted in party games, resented when visible. |
| DDA paradox | Adjustment helps while unnoticed but hurts autonomy or fairness once players notice or suspect it. | [S18, S19, S20] | QUALIFY. Name not from a paper; evidence small (n=21-42). |
| Placebo difficulty | Players' perceived difficulty and immersion shift when told a game adapts, even when it does not. | [S20] | ADOPT. Framing is a free tuning knob. |
| Player modelling | Detecting, modelling, predicting and expressing player traits or states from play data. | [S14, S15] | ADOPT. |
| Engagement-optimised matchmaking | Pairing players to minimise predicted churn rather than to equalise skill. | [S16] | QUALIFY. Vendor simulation; optimises retention, not fun. |
| Skill atom | Loop where the player acts, the game updates, shows feedback, and the player updates a mental model. | [S22] | ADOPT. Smallest unit of the learning curve. |
| Skill chain | Directed graph of skill atoms; later skills build on earlier ones. | [S22] | ADOPT. |
| Burnout (skill) | A learned skill that stops being useful, so players drop it and dependent skills wither. | [S22] | ADOPT. Not player fatigue. |
| Learning curve | What the player can do and understand over time. | [S24] | ADOPT. Never say "steep" without stating axes. |
| Sawtooth (introduce-practise-combine) | Each new skill enters alone and simply, then complexity rises until the next skill drops it back. | [S24] | ADOPT. Measured in puzzle games only. |
| Difficulty curve | The challenge the game presents over time, independent of player power. | [I] | ADOPT. Plot together with the power curve. |
| Power curve | The player's means over time (levels, talents, gear, Town). | [I] | ADOPT. Felt difficulty is difficulty vs power vs skill. |
| Interest curve | Plot of player interest across a session: hook, rising peaks with rests, final climax. | [S27] | ADOPT. Have playtesters draw it, not designers. |
| Tension and release | Alternating high-threat and low-threat periods. | [S17, S27] | ADOPT via the director cycle. |
| Perceived vs actual difficulty | How hard players feel the game is versus measured challenge; framing and attribution move the first. | [S8, S20, S29] | ADOPT. Measure both. |
| Four keys | Lazzaro's emotion sources: Hard Fun, Easy Fun, Altered States, People Factor. | [S23] | QUALIFY. Qualitative study, 30 players. |
| Fiero | Triumph felt after overcoming a hard obstacle. | [S23] | ADOPT. |
| Challenge types (CORGIS) | Performative, cognitive, emotional, decision-making challenge. | [S29] | ADOPT. Validated scale for playtest surveys. |
| SDT needs | Competence, autonomy, relatedness; satisfying them predicts enjoyment and continued play. | [S30] | ADOPT. |
| PENS | Survey of in-game competence, autonomy, relatedness, presence and intuitive controls. | [S30] | ADOPT for playtests. |
| Kishōtenketsu (level) | Four beats per level: introduce a gimmick, develop it, twist it, conclude. | [S26] | QUALIFY. A level structure, not a theory. |
| PLAY heuristics | Desurvire & Wiberg's playability checklist (game play, skill development, tutorial, challenge, immersion). | [S25] | QUALIFY. Checklist for reviews, not a curve model. |
| Yerkes-Dodson | Inverted U between arousal and performance. | [U] | AVOID unless citing the original; designers use it loosely. |
| "Fun" (bare) | Undefined. | [S10] | AVOID. Name the MDA aesthetic or key instead. |
