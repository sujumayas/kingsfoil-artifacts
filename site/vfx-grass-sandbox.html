Server and simulation

1 · Server grass truth

Each 8 px cell holds a growth stage (tall, regrowing, cut stubble), a burn state and a regrowth clock. Wind, season and weather come from a slow server clock. Only stage changes are sent, at 9 bytes each, the size of a TFS tile update. Cosmetic trampling stays on the client and costs 0 bytes. The HUD also shows what sending every flatten value would cost. A player in untrampled tall grass is hidden from everyone except party members, adjacent viewers (Chebyshev distance 1) and viewers in the same grass patch. The hide waits 400 ms after you enter, so it does not flicker at patch edges. Hidden players are not sent at all. Cutting or burning counts as an attack and reveals you for 2,000 ms, as League brush does. The sandbox has no party, so that case never fires. Send hidden players plays the TFS anti-pattern: an invisible player still goes out with an empty outfit, so the position leaks. You see yourself dithered.

2 · Ground tiles

The ground is baked once into a palette-index buffer and copied each frame. Grass tone comes from a position hash plus two noise scales, so there is no visible grid. Road, pond and path edges use a 2-corner rule: each pixel blends its four corner values and thresholds against a hash, which gives the same result as a 16-tile Wang set. Wear uses the same rule, so desire paths get ragged edges. Only 8 by 8 blocks whose state changed are rebaked.

3 · Tufts and y-sort

Tall grass, wheat, flowers and reeds are a few thousand tufts built in code at load. Every tuft is pre-baked at 13 whole-pixel leans, 3 crush levels, 4 growth heights and with a gust sheen: about 9,400 tiny pixel lists. Each tuft gets a hashed variant, an offset, a 50% flip and its own phase, as Stardew does with up to 4 tufts per tile. Tufts y-sort with characters, so a walker in tall grass loses the lower body behind the blades. One field layer draws all grass in a single shader-like pass instead. Characters then sit on top and their legs are cropped, Pokemon style.

4 · Wind and gusts

The model has two parts (Habel 2007). A slow, strong gust map is a cosine wave about 100 px long that travels downwind, inside a noise envelope. A weak flutter is two slow waves at 0.13 and 0.29 Hz. Crests run at 33 px/s in a breeze up to 170 px/s in a storm, Terraria's 3.0 s to 0.6 s period scaled to 8 px cells. Below 0.12 strength the grass stands still. Wheat bends most, then tall grass, flowers, reeds and short grass. Bent tips switch to one lighter palette step, so the gust reads as a sheen. Smooth sub-pixel is the anti-pattern: blended edge colours that smear and shimmer. Sway at 5 fps steps the sway like the Dynamic 2D Grass add-on.

5 · Interaction map

An 80 by 45 map at 4 px holds crush, push and bend direction for every walker (player, villager, sheep, rabbits). GDQuest uses 128 by 128 for a 16 m plane. Stamp strength equals the distance moved, so standing still leaves no new mark. The push peaks at 3 px and fades in 0.33 s (Terraria WindGrid). Each frame the map decays before the walkers stamp. The crush fades linearly and recovers in about 3 s through three poses: flat above 170, half above 60, then upright. Tufts keep 10% of their spring so they never freeze flat (Jahrmann and Wimmer 2017). Flat tufts are drawn as 1 to 3 px strokes, one palette step darker, lying in the walk direction, and the ground under a fresh trail darkens one step. Entering tall grass plays a 0.83 s rustle (Emerald: 4 frames of 10 ticks, then rest) and throws 4 leaves (Shattered Pixel Dungeon). Tufts with no wind, crush or rustle are settled and skip the lean math.

6 · Desire paths

Wear follows Helbing's trail model on one byte per 4 px cell: each footstep adds I·(1 - G/Gmax), and wear fades back toward zero with time constant T. Grass looks worn after about 20 steps and turns to dirt after about 50, the thresholds of the Step Erosion plugin. A path that nobody uses fades back. That takes 72 hours in Step Erosion and 3 minutes here, compressed 1:1440. Worn cells hide tufts and rebake the ground.

7 · Cut, burn, graze, regrow

The scythe turns a cell to stubble and throws clippings. A cricket jumps out 25% of the time and a frog 2% of the time (Stardew weeds). Fire uses the Fire study's grid rule: burning cells ignite neighbours with odds set by fuel, wind direction, rain and snow. Sheep graze one cell at a time. Regrowth is a random tick. Each tick picks N cells, a cell grows one stage with 65% odds, and a full cell spreads to a cut neighbour 25% of the time (Stardew). With TFS (2 stages of 240 s) and Luanti (about 300 s per tile) as reference, the default here is compressed about 20x.

8 · Small life

All creatures live in one pooled struct-of-arrays and are culled off screen. Butterflies use Stardew flight: each flap kicks them up and sideways, gravity pulls between flaps, and they rest 200 ms after 4 or 6 flap frames. Ours also land on flowers and flee. Bees hop between flowers about every 2 s. Dragonflies hover, then dart at 10x the speed. Midges are springs pulled toward a centre. Moths orbit the lamp, stall and flip. Rabbits run once a threat is within 5 cells and dive into cover. Frogs leap for 0.8 s into the pond. Critters check for threats every 200 ms, not every frame. Rules from Stardew and Terraria: no butterflies after 15:00 (ours taper), in rain or in a storm, and frogs come out after rain.

9 · Fireflies

The default model is pulse coupling (Nicky Case's shape), not Kuramoto. Each fly's phase rises at 1/period, with the period 5.9 s ± 10% (P. pyralis). At 1 the fly flashes and resets, and every neighbour within 48 px gets phase += phase·0.035. A uniform 48 px grid finds the neighbours, and the work only happens on flash frames. A flash rises in 1 frame and falls over 0.4 s through 4 halo frames, with a J-shaped rise. Synchronous switches to P. carolinus: bursts of 6 flashes 0.5 s apart, then about 9 s of darkness, with the same coupling. The glow is a pre-made halo that raises palette light levels. It is not a per-pixel light. Godot Compatibility allows at most 15 lights per canvas item, so one light per fly is not possible there. Each night rolls a density: heavy about 1 in 9, empty about 1 in 3 (Terraria). Fireflies stay out about 3 h after dusk.

10 · Seasons, weather, access

The season is a palette swap. The grass ramps are anchored on Stardew's hues, spring 119°, summer 89° and fall 16°, with yellower lights and bluer shadows. A per-cell phenology offset makes the field turn over 8 s instead of all at once. Winter adds snow, so only tufts' tips poke through, the pond freezes and the trees go bare. After rain, grass gets darker and more saturated, with glints and road puddles. Reduce motion stops field sway completely, because guidelines flag repeated motion over 25% of the screen. Density thins creatures. Numbers not tied to a source above are illustrative.

11 · What it costs

Every layer is timed with performance.now() each frame and smoothed. Everything writes palette indices into one buffer, and one lookup table turns them into colours. The study's benchmark found this path fastest. For 2,000 tufts, writing pixels straight into one buffer costs 0.051 ms. One Canvas2D drawImage per tuft costs 1.62 ms. Slicing each tuft per row at draw time costs 12 ms, so never do that. An 80 by 45 trail map with 10 walkers costs 0.0023 ms. A random-tick pick costs about 3 ns. Firefly sync at 1,000 flies costs 1.14 ms naive, 0.42 ms with a grid and 0.067 ms with a per-cell mean field. A CPU halo loop beats additive sprites, 0.22 ms against 0.89 ms for 1,000 halos. A full frame at default settings measured about 0.4 ms on a desktop and 2.3 to 2.6 ms with a 6x CPU throttle. The HUD total includes the wind field, the x-ray overlay and the canvas upload. Stress multiplies tufts and creatures up to 8x so you can see where the budget breaks. Cost view colours each 8 px cell by the pixel writes and updates it caused.
