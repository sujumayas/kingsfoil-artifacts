X-ray view (the data behind the pixels)

1 · One byte of depth per pixel

The ground keeps a snow-depth map (Uint8Array(320*180)). Every frame, snowfall adds 1 unit at about 900 random pixels. Each pick is kept with probability equal to that pixel's exposure, so open ground fills first and sheltered ground fills last. Snowfall stops adding once a pixel holds what its exposure allows, about 110 units in the open, so a long storm keeps its drifts and hollows.

2 · Dither-threshold coverage

Every pixel has a fixed threshold: a 4×4 Bayer value plus noise, plus extra for grass tufts. Snow shows where depth > threshold. Thin snow therefore appears as an ordered dusting, and melt pulls back as dithered patches. No tiles are swapped, so nothing pops.

3 · Shelter = cheap rays

Exposure is baked once per wind bucket. Each ground pixel steps up to 28 pixels upwind; if it meets a tall object, it sits in that object's lee. Just past the lee there is a +12% drift band. Pixels under tree canopies and house eaves get a fixed shelter factor, so bare rings show under the trees.

4 · Footprints move snow, they don't paint it

A boot sets the depth under it to 30% and pushes the rest into the rim, so total snow is conserved. The core is marked packed. New snowfall refills prints and erases the packed mark, which makes old trails soften and fade. Characters also sink into deep snow, and it slows them.

5 · A sandpile pass keeps it soft

Every frame, 4,000 random cells compare depth with one neighbour. If the difference is more than 3, one unit slides over (an angle-of-repose rule). Wind moves loose snow downwind, and it stops against walls and fences. That's where drifts come from.

6 · Melt eats edges, shade lasts longest

Above 0 °C, random cells lose depth. Rate = temperature × sun × (0.25 in cast shadow) × (0.5 if packed) × (more open neighbours = faster). Melted snow becomes a wet value that darkens the ground and then evaporates. Snow survives longest on the north side of things and in packed trails, as it does outdoors.

7 · Palette lighting, Doom-style

The world renders into an 8-bit index buffer. The day/night tint is applied to the roughly 240-entry palette once per frame, not to 57,600 pixels. The lamp's warm light is a second palette chosen per pixel through a Bayer threshold, so you get dithered pools of light.

8 · Flakes in 3 depth layers

Each flake has ground (x, y) plus a height z and is drawn at (x, y−z). Far flakes are 1 px and slow; near flakes are 2 px, fast, and cast a shadow dot on the ground. When z reaches 0 the flake adds depth exactly where it lands.
